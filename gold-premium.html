Gold Premium Explained: Why Physical Gold Costs More Than the Spot Price

The gold premium is the extra amount you pay for physical gold on top of the international spot price. It differs from product to product and from country to country. This page explains what it is, what drives it and how to read it.

What is the gold premium?

Gold is quoted all day in financial markets. That quote is the spot price. The price you actually pay for a bar or coin at a dealer is almost always higher. The difference is the gold premium. It is usually stated per troy ounce or as a percentage of the spot price.

Premium = price paid per ounce − spot price per ounce

Premium % = premium ÷ spot price × 100

Illustrative numbers, not market data: with spot at $4,000 per ounce and a dealer price of $4,120 for a one-ounce bar, the premium is $120 or 3.0 %.

Spot price vs. physical price

The spot price is a wholesale benchmark. It reflects the price for large, standardised bars (typically around 400 troy ounces) traded between banks and bullion dealers. A private buyer wants something else: a smaller bar, a coin, delivered to a specific country and sold with VAT or duties. Each of those steps costs money, and the premium is where those costs show up.

What drives the premium?

  • Product size and type. Small bars and coins carry a higher percentage premium than large bars, because fabrication and handling cost more per ounce.
  • Refiner, mint and brand. Well-known brands and government mints often trade at a higher price, and they are usually easier to resell.
  • Dealer margin and competition. Markets with many competing dealers tend to trade closer to spot.
  • Shipping, insurance and storage. Moving and protecting metal is not free.
  • Taxes and duties. Import duties, VAT or consumption taxes can change the price you pay considerably from one country to another.
  • Local supply and demand. Strong local demand or a shortage of physical metal pushes premiums up.
  • Currency. Prices in local currency also move with exchange rates, which matters when you compare markets.
  • Market stress. In tight markets premiums can widen sharply, and the same one-kilo bar can cost noticeably different amounts depending on where it is bought.

Why premiums differ between countries

No two gold markets work in exactly the same way. Import rules, tax treatment, the number of dealers and local buying habits all differ. Markets with import restrictions or strong domestic demand have historically shown wider premiums, while mature, competitive dealer markets tend to stay closer to spot.

How to read a premium correctly

  • Compare like with like. Use the same product size and the same unit (ounce, gram or kilogram).
  • Check whether tax is included. A price with VAT or duty is not comparable to one without it. GoldPremium.org notes where a quoted price includes tax or duty.
  • Look at both sides. Dealers buy back at lower prices than they sell. The gap between buy and sell price is a cost on top of the premium.
  • Mind the timing. Prices change during the day, so only prices taken at about the same time are comparable.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal gold premium?
There is no single number. It depends on the product, the market and the moment. As a rough orientation, large bars carry a smaller percentage premium than small bars and coins.
Is a high premium always bad for the buyer?
Not necessarily. A high premium means you pay more over spot, but it can reflect real costs such as taxes, shipping or scarce supply. What matters is what you get for it and what you can sell it for later.
Can the premium be negative?
A price below spot can occur in some markets or segments, for example when local supply is plentiful. It is the exception.
Does the premium also apply when I sell?
Usually not in the same way. Dealers typically buy back at a lower price than they sell, so the buy-sell gap matters in addition to the premium.
Why is the gold price in my country different from the spot price?
Spot is a global wholesale benchmark in US dollars. Local prices add the premium and are expressed in local currency, so exchange rates play a part too.
